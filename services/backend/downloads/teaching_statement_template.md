# Teaching Statement Template

A teaching statement should read as a philosophy backed by concrete
evidence, not a list of adjectives ("I am passionate about..."). Committees
read dozens of these; specificity is what separates a memorable one from a
forgettable one. Aim for 1-2 pages.

## 1. Teaching philosophy

State your core beliefs about how people in your field actually learn, and
why you teach the way you do as a result. Ground abstract claims in a
concrete classroom moment:
- What do you want students to be able to *do*, not just know, after your
  course?
- How do you handle the range of prior preparation in a typical classroom?
- What's one belief about teaching you hold that isn't universal in your
  field, and why do you hold it?

## 2. Teaching experience and evidence

List courses you've taught or TA'd, at what level, and what you actually
did in them (redesigned a lab, introduced a project component, rewrote
assessments). Where you have it, cite evidence: student evaluation scores,
a specific piece of student feedback, or an outcome you can point to
(improved pass rates, a project that went on to a publication or startup).
Absence of formal teaching experience is fine — describe mentoring,
workshops, or TA work instead, and say what you'd do differently as
instructor of record.

## 3. Course offerings and development

- **Courses you're prepared to teach now:** core/service courses in your
  department's existing curriculum.
- **New courses you'd develop:** 1-2 specific proposals connecting your
  research to teaching — give a course title and one-sentence description,
  not just a topic area.

## 4. Mentorship

How do you mentor undergraduate researchers, graduate students, or interns?
What's your approach to advising someone whose background or goals differ
from your own path? If you've mentored students from underrepresented
groups in your field, describe what you actually did, not just that you
value diversity in the abstract.

## 5. Inclusive and accessible teaching

Concrete practices, not slogans: how do you structure a course so students
without your background can catch up? How do you handle accommodations,
group work dynamics, or a large gap in preparation within one classroom?

## Common mistakes to avoid
- Philosophy with no evidence it's ever been applied.
- Claiming to teach "for all learning styles" without specifics.
- New-course proposals so vague they could be taught by anyone.
- No mention of how you'd handle a struggling or disengaged student.
