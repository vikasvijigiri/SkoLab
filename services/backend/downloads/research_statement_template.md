# Research Statement Template

A research statement for a tenure-track application does three things: shows
a reviewer you have a coherent research *program* (not a list of unrelated
papers), proves you can execute it (funding, students, infrastructure), and
convinces a hiring committee outside your subfield why the work matters.
Aim for 2-4 pages. Every section below is a prompt, not a rule — cut what
doesn't apply to your field.

## 1. Opening: the one-paragraph pitch

Before any detail, state the problem you work on, why it matters, and what
makes your approach distinctive. A committee member skimming 150
applications should get your whole research identity from this paragraph
alone. Avoid jargon here even if your field is technical — assume a smart
reader from an adjacent department.

## 2. Completed work

Organize by research *theme*, not chronologically by paper. For each theme:
- The question you asked and why it was open.
- Your specific contribution (method, result, dataset, tool) — be concrete,
  not just "we studied X."
- The impact so far: citations, adoption, follow-on work by others, or a
  concrete real-world use, whichever is honest and true for your case.
- 1-2 representative publications (full citation list goes in your CV, not
  here).

## 3. Current work in progress

What are you working on right now that isn't published yet? This tells the
committee your pipeline isn't empty the moment you finish the papers listed
above. Be honest about what's preliminary versus near-complete.

## 4. Future research plan (3-5 years)

This is the section committees weigh most heavily — it's where you show you
can lead a lab, not just execute someone else's agenda.
- **Near-term (year 1-2):** Concrete, fundable, likely to produce your first
  papers as a PI. Should connect clearly to your completed work.
- **Medium-term (year 2-4):** A larger, riskier direction that needs the
  infrastructure/team you'll build in year 1-2.
- **Long-term / vision:** Where does this go if it succeeds? One paragraph,
  ambitious but not fantastical.
For each, name the funding mechanism you'd target (NSF CAREER, NIH R01,
DOE Early Career, industry gift, etc.) — it signals you've done your
homework on how the work gets paid for.

## 5. Fit with the department

One short paragraph: which existing faculty, courses, centers, or shared
equipment does your plan build on or complement? This is the section most
applicants skip and it's an easy way to stand out — it shows you read the
department, not just the job ad.

## Common mistakes to avoid
- Writing a list of papers instead of a narrative arc.
- A future-plans section that's really just "more of the same."
- No mention of students, funding, or how the work actually gets done.
- Statements only your closest collaborators could parse.
